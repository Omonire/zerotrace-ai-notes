AI Notes is an open source note-taking application built with Python and Flask.
It stores every note in a local SQLite database so that the person running it
keeps full ownership of their own data. The goal is simple: give people a fast,
private, hackable place to write things down and think.

A note is a small thing. It has a title, a body, a color, and a handful of tags.
You can create a note, edit it, search across it, and delete it when you no
longer need it. Tags make it easy to group related ideas without a rigid folder
structure, and colors give each note a quick visual identity in the list.

The assistant is the part that makes the app feel alive. Ask it to summarize a
long note and it returns the essential points. Ask it to expand a rough outline
and it fills in the missing detail. Ask it to rewrite a paragraph in a different
register and the tone shifts. The assistant reads your notes as context, so its
answers stay grounded in what you actually wrote.

Language models do not understand words the way people do. They read text as a
sequence of tokens and learn to predict the next token from the ones before it.
Train the model on enough text and a remarkable thing happens: to predict the
next token well it must internalize grammar, facts, and a little bit of
reasoning. The transformer is the architecture that made this practical.

The transformer reads the whole sequence at once instead of one step at a time.
Each token is turned into a vector, and every token asks a question of every
other token: which of you are relevant to me right now? Those questions are the
queries, the answers are the keys, and the information that flows back is the
value. This is attention, and it lets the model weigh distant words as easily as
near ones.

Because the model must never see the future when predicting the next token, the
attention is masked. A lower triangular mask hides every position after the
current one, so the prediction for a given token depends only on the tokens that
came before it. This single constraint turns a general sequence encoder into a
language model that can generate text one token at a time.

Stack a few of these blocks together, add layer normalization, residual
connections, and a feed-forward network, and you have a decoder. Feed it a
prompt, sample from its probability distribution, append the sampled token, and
repeat. That loop is how a language model writes. Temperature controls how
adventurous the sampling is, while top-k and top-p trim the unlikely tail.

Training is a loop of its own. Take a batch of text, split it into inputs and
targets, run a forward pass, compute the cross-entropy loss, run backpropagation,
and nudge every weight a little in the direction that lowers the loss. Do this
many thousands of times and the loss falls. The model starts by producing noise
and ends by producing sentences that sound like the corpus it studied.

The best way to understand any of this is to build it. Write the tokenizer by
hand. Write attention by hand. Watch the loss curve bend downward and sample a
few tokens to see what the model has learned so far. Reading about a transformer
is useful; training one, even a tiny one, is unforgettable.

Notes are personal. A note might be a shopping list, a fragment of a song, a
plan for the week, or the first sentence of a novel. The job of a good tool is
to stay out of the way and let the words arrive. Everything else, the database,
the search, the assistant, is there to serve that quiet moment of thinking.
